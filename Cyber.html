Case 1 • High Priority • Server Troubleshooting

Printers Not Working

The first scenario involved a building-wide printer outage. The reporting user explained that none of the printers throughout the building were working, making this a high priority issue because of its potential business impact.

The first step was identity verification. The user was contacted through the ticket workflow and provided a 2FA code that could be checked against the employee directory. Once the identity was confirmed, the scope of the outage became important: multiple users and multiple floors were affected.

Because the problem was widespread, the investigation moved away from an individual workstation and toward the server environment. The server-room view showed the print server experiencing an outage. Restarting the server restored the printing service.

What I took away
  • Verify the user before making changes.
  • Consider the scope of an outage before troubleshooting an individual endpoint.
  • Check shared infrastructure when multiple users experience the same failure.
  • Verify the solution with the reporting user before closing the ticket.

Case 2 • High Priority • Remote Support

Incorrect Clock on User End

This case involved a user whose computer clock was set to the wrong time zone. The mismatch was causing confusion and had already resulted in missed meetings.

After verifying the user's identity, the appropriate response was to remotely access the workstation because the user had already identified the likely solution: correcting the system's time-zone settings.

From the remote desktop, the settings were opened and the Time and Language section was used to change the system from Eastern Time to Central Time. The computer was then synced to confirm that the correction had taken effect.

What I took away
  • Verify identity before remote access.
  • Use remote support when the problem can be resolved directly and efficiently.
  • Confirm that system settings match the user's actual environment.

Case 3 • Medium Priority • Directory Administration

New Employee

The third ticket involved creating an account for a new employee. The request contained the information needed to create the account and also specified that the new employee would need to be assigned to appropriate groups.

After verifying the reporting user's identity, the work remained within the employee directory. The new account was created using the supplied information, with particular attention paid to correctly entering names and other account details.

Once the account appeared in the directory, the user's group memberships were updated to match the requirements of the employee's role.

What I took away
  • Verify the request before creating an account.
  • Accuracy matters when creating user records.
  • Account creation and group assignment are separate parts of the workflow.

Case 4 • Medium Priority • Access Management

Moved Teams and Cannot Access New Resources

This scenario involved an employee moving to a new team and therefore requiring new access while having previous access removed.

Unlike the previous cases, this ticket involved two people: the manager submitting the request and the employee whose access was being changed. Both identities needed to be verified before making changes.

Once both users were verified, the directory was used to add the employee to the required group and remove the previous group. The exercise also highlighted an important distinction between permissions that can be added immediately and removals that require confirmation.

What I took away
  • Verify all involved parties when a request affects another user's access.
  • Understand the difference between granting and revoking access.
  • Follow the organization's expected order of operations when modifying permissions.

Case 5 • Low Priority • Account Administration

Legal Name Change

The final scenario involved a user who needed their last name changed following a marriage. Because the organization's email format was tied to the user's name, the email address also needed to be updated.

After verifying the user's identity, the directory profile was opened. The display name was updated, a new email address reflecting the change was assigned, and the previous address was configured as an alias.

The change could be observed directly after saving the profile, making this a good example of a ticket where the technician can immediately see the result rather than relying on a separate technical verification.

What I took away
  • Verify identity before changing account information.
  • Consider dependencies such as email addresses when changing user information.
  • Preserve the previous email as an alias when required.
  • Confirm the change and communicate clearly with the user.

Where It Started

Narrowing the Scope

My technical support experience started well before I moved into software and systems engineering. As a Senior Technician at Valvoline Instant Oil Change, I worked directly with customers and internal teams to resolve hardware, software, and technology issues.

That meant troubleshooting peripherals, coordinating equipment replacement and inventory, working with vendors and Internet service providers, and escalating problems to corporate teams when an issue required additional support.

The part of that experience that has stayed with me most is the troubleshooting process itself. A problem rarely arrives with a perfect description of what is wrong. You have to ask questions, gather information, narrow the possibilities, make a change, and then determine whether the change actually solved the problem.

I still approach engineering problems in much the same way. Whether I'm debugging a container, investigating a networking problem, working with a remote Linux environment, or dealing with unfamiliar hardware, I start by understanding the environment before trying to fix it.

Continuing Development

Building More Service Desk Experience

I'm continuing to sharpen my technical support and troubleshooting skills through hands-on work with a service desk simulator. The goal isn't simply to complete simulated tickets. I'm using the scenarios to practice the habits that make troubleshooting useful in a real environment.

That includes identifying the scope of a problem, verifying users, gathering information, working through the available systems, documenting what changed, and communicating the result. I'm documenting that process here so the page can grow alongside the work.
